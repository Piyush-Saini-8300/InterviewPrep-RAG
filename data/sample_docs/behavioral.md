# Behavioral Interview Notes

## Frameworks

### Q: [easy] Tell me about yourself.
Give a focused two-minute story rather than a life history, using a present, past, future structure. Start with who you are now and your current focus, such as the course or role and the skills you are building. Then highlight two or three relevant past experiences, projects or achievements that show impact, ideally with a number. Finish with why you are excited about this opportunity and how it connects to your goals. Keep it tailored to the job and avoid personal details that are not relevant.

### Q: [medium] What is the STAR method and why does it work?
STAR is a structure for answering behavioral questions: Situation, Task, Action and Result. Briefly set the situation and your task, spend most of the answer on the specific actions you personally took, and end with the result, quantified where possible, plus what you learned. It works because it keeps answers concrete, concise and focused on your own contribution instead of vague generalities. Prepare five or six stories that can be adapted to themes such as leadership, conflict, failure, ownership and deadlines.

## Common questions

### Q: [medium] Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a teammate.
Choose a real but professional disagreement and show maturity. Describe the situation neutrally without blaming the other person. Explain that you listened to understand their perspective, found the shared goal, and proposed an approach such as testing both ideas, agreeing on criteria, or involving a mentor for a decision. Finish with the outcome and the relationship afterwards, and what you learned about communication. Interviewers look for empathy, directness and a focus on the project rather than on winning.

### Q: [medium] Describe a time you failed or made a mistake.
Pick a genuine mistake with real but recoverable consequences, not a disguised strength. Explain what happened and take ownership without making excuses. Focus on how you responded: how you fixed the problem, communicated early with those affected, and what process or habit you changed so it would not happen again, such as adding tests, code reviews or checklists. Interviewers are evaluating self-awareness, accountability and the ability to learn.

### Q: [hard] How do you prioritise when you have multiple tight deadlines?
Explain a repeatable method. Clarify what is truly urgent versus important with the stakeholders, estimate effort and impact, and break work into small deliverables. Use a framework such as an impact and effort matrix or the Eisenhower matrix, then communicate the plan and trade-offs early, negotiate scope or dates if something has to give, and ask for help when needed. Track progress daily and adjust. Give a concrete example where you delivered the most valuable items on time through clear communication rather than working in silence.

### Q: [easy] Why do you want to work at this company, and what questions do you have for us?
Show that you did your research. Connect the company's products, mission or engineering culture to your own interests and skills with specific details, not generic praise. For your questions, ask about the team's current challenges, how success is measured in the first six months, how engineers receive feedback and grow, and what the technical roadmap looks like. Avoid asking about things easily found on the website, and avoid opening with salary or perks.
